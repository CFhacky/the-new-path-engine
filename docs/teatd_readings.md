# The End and the Death — a reader's companion

*Readings of Dan Abnett's trilogy for someone who has not read it, or has not read the sixty-odd books before it.*

**The End and the Death** is the three-volume finale of the **Horus Heresy**, a series of novels set in the Warhammer 40,000 universe that began in 2006 with Abnett's own *Horus Rising*. It tells the last hours of a civil war between a god-like father and his favourite son. This companion gathers a set of interpretations worked out in conversation between 21 and 24 September 2026. It is written so that a reader who knows neither the books nor the setting can follow every one.

**How it is laid out.**

- **Part One** gives everything you need first: what Warhammer 40,000 is, how the Heresy happened, a detailed retelling of all three volumes, the rules of the universe, who's who, and how the books are written.
- **Part Two** gives fifteen readings. Each shows the scene it rests on (**In the story**), the interpretation (**The reading**), and the myth, scripture or literature it draws on, told for someone who has not read that either.
- **The Appendix** lists every realm of the warp the trilogy names, with its source.

**How to read the labels.**

- Every interpretation is a reading, not a fact. Abnett does not footnote, so the mythic sources are inferred. He does say, in his afterword, that there is "scarcely a line, or at least a paragraph, in the whole book that doesn't make some nod to something in the lore."
- Every quotation from the trilogy has been checked word for word against the text, and against the verse it is cited to. Each carries an address like **TEATD3 9:xxi**, meaning Volume III, chapter 9, verse xxi. **F** is the final part. The addresses resolve in this repository with `scripts/verse_address.py`. Quotations from Abnett's afterword are cited "(Afterword, TEATD3)."
- Background marked **Series background** comes from the wider Horus Heresy series or the Warhammer setting, not from the three volumes. It is general knowledge of the setting and has not been checked against those books here.
- Where an interpretation began as the discussion partner's own thesis, it is marked **Thesis raised in discussion**, with the analysis that tested it underneath.
- Where a later check of the text overturned something an earlier version of these readings said, a **Correction** note says so.

# Part One — What you need to know first

This part is long on purpose. The trilogy is the last chapter of a story told across more than sixty books, and it assumes you know all of them. Nothing in Part Two will land without this.

## 1. What Warhammer 40,000 is

*Series background.*

**Warhammer 40,000** is a science-fiction war game made by Games Workshop, a British company based in Nottingham. It first appeared in 1987 as *Rogue Trader*. Players collect armies of small painted miniatures and fight battles on a tabletop. Around the game grew a fictional universe of enormous depth, and around the universe grew a publishing arm, **Black Library**, which has put out hundreds of novels.

The setting is the forty-first millennium. Humanity has spread across a million worlds under the **Imperium of Man**, a galactic empire that is simultaneously a theocracy, a bureaucracy and a war machine. Its slogan, printed on every box, is "In the grim darkness of the far future there is only war," and its tone gave the English language the word *grimdark*.

The Imperium's god is the **Emperor of Mankind**. He sits on the **Golden Throne** on **Terra**, the old Earth. He is not dead, but he has not moved or spoken in ten thousand years. The Throne is an ancient machine that keeps his ruined body alive, and it is fed a thousand psychics a day. His mind, still active in the warp, projects the **Astronomican**, a psychic lighthouse by which starships navigate between the stars. A vast church, the **Ecclesiarchy**, worships him. An **Inquisition** burns heretics in his name.

The original joke, and it began as a joke, came out of the same British satirical tradition as the comic *2000 AD* and *Judge Dredd*. It asked: what if the church were right? What if the gods were real, prayer worked, hell was a place you could fly into, and the only thing standing between humanity and damnation was a corpse on a chair that everyone worshipped and nobody was allowed to question? The universe is a satire on empire, faith, bureaucracy and war that takes its own premise with total seriousness. That combination is what makes it strange, and what makes it capable of the kind of fiction discussed here.

## 2. The Horus Heresy: how the Emperor ended up on the Throne

*Series background.*

The forty-first millennium is the present of the game. The **Horus Heresy** is its founding catastrophe, set ten thousand years earlier, at the end of the thirty-first millennium. It is the story of how the Emperor was betrayed by his favourite son, and how he came to be the thing on the Throne.

For decades the Heresy existed only as a few paragraphs of legend in the game's rulebooks, deliberately vague and contradictory. In 2006 Black Library began telling it in full, starting with Dan Abnett's *Horus Rising*. The **Horus Heresy** novel series ran to more than fifty books by a rotating team of authors, followed by a closing sequence of ten, **The Siege of Terra** (2019–2024). The last three books of the Siege are *The End and the Death*, Volumes I, II and III, all by Abnett, published in 2023 and 2024. Abnett wrote the first book of the saga and the last.

### The Emperor before the Heresy

The Emperor's origins are a mystery the setting keeps on purpose. The most common account says he was born in prehistoric Anatolia, many thousands of years before the present day, and that he walked through human history in secret, under many names and faces, steering it. In the ages that followed, humanity reached the stars. Then the **Age of Strife** came: warp storms cut every world off from every other, and civilisation collapsed into what the books call Old Night.

The Emperor emerged openly at its end. He conquered a shattered Terra in the **Unification Wars**, allied with the machine-priests of Mars, and began the **Great Crusade** to reunite humanity across the galaxy. He did it under a doctrine called the **Imperial Truth**: there are no gods, religion is superstition, and humanity will be ruled by reason and science. The trilogy's own concluding irony rests on that doctrine.

### The primarchs and the Legions

The Emperor's instruments were twenty genetically engineered sons, the **primarchs**. They were superhuman in every respect, and each had a distinctive gift and character. In their infancy they were scattered across the galaxy by a catastrophe the traditional account blames on the Chaos gods. The Emperor found them one by one during the Crusade.

From each primarch's genetic material, his **gene-seed**, the Emperor made a **Legion** of **Space Marines**, also called **Astartes**: thousands of super-soldiers, each a lesser copy of his primarch. Every Legion inherited its father's strengths and flaws.

## 3. How the Heresy happened

*Series background. The names of the novels that tell each event are given in brackets.*

- **Horus made Warmaster.** After a great victory at **Ullanor**, the Emperor returned to Terra and handed command of the Crusade to Horus, the first son he had found and the one he loved most. Horus was made **Warmaster**. The Emperor never told his sons why he was leaving. [*Horus Rising*]
- **The ban on sorcery.** At the **Council of Nikaea** the Emperor ruled on whether the Legions could use psychic powers. Magnus the Red argued for knowledge and lost, and the use of sorcery was forbidden. [*A Thousand Sons*]
- **The rebuke of faith.** Lorgar, primarch of the Word Bearers, had begun to worship his father as a god. On the world of **Monarchia** the Emperor had Lorgar's Legion kneel in the ashes of a city built to his glory and told them he was not a god. The humiliation turned Lorgar and his priest **Erebus** toward other gods, who were happy to be worshipped. [*The First Heretic*]
- **Davin.** On the moon of **Davin**, Horus was wounded by a cursed blade. Erebus had him taken to a temple of a warrior lodge, where the Chaos powers showed him visions: the Emperor, they said, would make himself a god and discard his sons. Horus woke believing it. [*False Gods*]
- **Isstvan.** Horus purged the loyal officers of his own Legion and three others by virus-bombing the world of **Isstvan III**. Garviel Loken, a captain of the Luna Wolves and the hero of *Horus Rising*, survived. At **Isstvan V** the loyal Legions sent to punish Horus were ambushed by traitors posing as allies, and Ferrus Manus was killed by his brother Fulgrim. [*Galaxy in Flames*, *Fulgrim*]
- **Prospero and the webway.** Magnus learned of Horus's treachery and warned his father by sorcery, the thing he had been forbidden to use. The spell struck Terra through a secret project beneath the Emperor's Palace. The Emperor had been extending humanity into the **webway**, an ancient alien network of tunnels outside the warp, to free starships from the warp's dangers. Magnus's spell broke the project's wards and daemons poured into the tunnels. The Emperor had to take his seat on the **Golden Throne**, the project's control engine, to hold the breach shut. Leman Russ burned Magnus's homeworld **Prospero**, and Magnus gave himself to Tzeentch, god of change. [*A Thousand Sons*, *Prospero Burns*, *The Master of Mankind*]
- **Molech.** Horus led his fleet to **Molech**, a world with a gate into the warp. The books suggest that the Emperor had passed through that gate long ago, made some bargain with the dark powers, and taken power from them without paying for it. Horus went through and came back with the power of a god. [*Vengeful Spirit*]
- **The Siege of Terra.** Horus brought the traitor Legions to Terra and besieged the Emperor's Palace. The loyal defence was commanded by Rogal Dorn, primarch of the Imperial Fists, with Valdor, the Custodes, the Blood Angels of Sanguinius, the White Scars of Jaghatai Khan, and the mortal armies of Terra. Malcador held the administration together. Through seven Siege novels the Palace is ground down, wall by wall. [*The Solar War*, *The Lost and the Damned*, *The First Wall*, *Saturnine*, *Mortis*, *Warhawk*, *Echoes of Eternity*]

The Siege ends in *The End and the Death*.

## 4. What happens in The End and the Death

*The trilogy itself. Every scene below is retold from the text, with addresses so it can be found.*

### Volume I: the choice

**The Throne Room.** The book opens underground, in the chamber of the Golden Throne beneath the Emperor's Palace, in what Malcador calls "this last hour of the final act." The Emperor has sat on the Throne for years, holding shut the breach Magnus made in the webway, in constant pain: "Though the room is quiet, the warp howls in his ear" (TEATD1 1:v). Malcador calls to him silently, mind to mind, and reflects that in thousands of years at his side he has never seen his lord's real face: "No one has ever seen his true face, or learned his true name" (TEATD1 1:v). The Emperor hates the look of the thing he has become, "a golden king, idle upon a golden throne" (TEATD1 1:v).

**The last allies.** The Emperor says, "*I cannot fight alone*," and Malcador sends his mind out to summon those who must be part of the end (TEATD1 1:xxiii).
- **Vulkan** is found deep in the webway, a charred skeleton with a hammer, dying and reviving over and over. He has just killed Magnus. Malcador asks him to come home and bar the webway door.
- **Rogal Dorn** is exhausted: he has run the whole defence and has no moves left.
- **Sanguinius** is hiding grave wounds. He has just defeated the daemon Ka'Bandha and the traitor primarch Angron, and sealed the Eternity Gate, the last great door of the Palace.
- **Constantin Valdor** is hunting daemons in the Palace with the Apollonian Spear, a weapon that learns from everything it kills. Malcador knows Valdor has secretly allowed a weapon to be built that would kill every primarch and every Space Marine, and has let it happen.

"These four are the last of them. The rest are all dead, or have become the reason that our world is dying" (TEATD1 1:xxiii).

**Oll's journey.** Far below, **Oll Persson** leads his small band up through the buried ruins of the civilisations the Palace was built on (TEATD1 1:xvi). With him are:
- a latent psychic, a soldier and a farmhand;
- **John Grammaticus**;
- **Leetu**;
- **Actae**, the blind prophetess;
- an Alpha Legion guide.

Oll carries the **athame**, an ancient stone knife he took from the Word Bearers, "the instrument of the original murder, splashed with the blood of Abel" (TEATD1 1:xvi). It has killed seven times and "been promised an eighth death." He means to reach the Emperor.

**The shields are down.** Malcador, scanning the war across the Solar System, sees a detail so small he doubts it. The Emperor confirms it in his mind: "+It is true, Sigillite.+" Horus's flagship "the *Vengeful Spirit*, his first-found's death ship, has lowered its shields" (TEATD1 1:xxxi). It may be damage, a challenge, or "a vulgar trap." It does not matter: "There is our variable." The traitor's heart is open to attack.

**The sacrifice.** The Emperor's armour and sword, unworn since the Great Crusade, are brought out. Sanguinius has had visions he hides, and the Emperor tells Malcador what they are: "+Can you not guess, Malcador? He has seen me fall.+" (TEATD1 2:ix). Someone must hold the Throne while the Emperor goes to war, and it can only be Malcador. He has prepared for it "since the day we realised that Magnus was no longer a viable candidate" (TEATD1 2:xvi). The Throne was meant to be Magnus's: "The contingency's name was Magnus" (TEATD1 2:xvi).

The Emperor rises from the Throne for the first time in years, and his sons fall to their knees. Malcador, old and lame, climbs the steps, draws his sigil on the breastplates of the two Custodians who helped him up, Caecaltus Dusk and Uzkarel Ophite, asking them to bring their lord back alive, and sits down: "I sit upon the Golden Throne" (TEATD1 2:xvi). The Throne starts to kill him at once. Vulkan can feel "his cells dying, one by one." When Vulkan names him, the Emperor corrects him: "+The Hero.+" (TEATD1 2:xviii).

**Anabasis.** Word goes to the command centre: the flagship's "void shields are down. He has risen to lead it Himself. Operational reference is *Anabasis*" (TEATD1 2:xix). *Anabasis* is Greek for "the march up-country," the title of Xenophon's account of a doomed expedition into Persia. The Emperor, Dorn, Sanguinius, Valdor and the elite of their Legions teleport aboard the *Vengeful Spirit*.

### Volume II: the price

**The ship becomes hell.** Aboard the flagship there is no ship left. The *Vengeful Spirit* has become a warp-maze fused with the Inevitable City, and eventually with Terra itself. Horus, narrated in the second person, has scattered the four intruders, "Constantin. Your siblings, Rogal and Sanguinius. Your father" (TEATD2 5:ii), and prepared a tempting "gift" from each Chaos god for each of them.
- **Valdor and his Custodians** are trapped in a pit of flesh, counting a battle that lasts days in seconds.
- **Dorn** is lost for what feels like centuries in a red desert under a red wall. A voice asks him, "*Who is the blood for? Just say it*" (TEATD2 8:xvi), which is the war god's question.
- **Sanguinius** forces his way on alone. In a crypt he meets the ghost of his dead brother Ferrus Manus.
- **Loken** hunts through the ship's depths, pursued by a daemon.

**Malcador burns.** On the Throne, Malcador narrates his own destruction: "Almost all of me is gone now" (TEATD2 5:viii). Every part of him has been burned away, "even the name *Malcador*." Looking into the unleashed warp, he gives the trilogy its key idea: that humanity's idea of hell "is derived from the warp" (TEATD2 5:viii). He seems to die, then does not. To keep him alive, Vulkan orders the "Unspoken Sanction," bringing in psychically able candidates to "support and fortify the Sigillite at whatever cost is necessary" (TEATD2 6:vii), decisions Malcador calls "ghastly" and says "will haunt him for the rest of his life." Malcador: "I died the moment I sat down, but I am not yet dead" (TEATD2 6:viii).

**The Emperor becomes the Dark King.** The Chaos gods throw at the Emperor "a condensed paroxysm that exceeds all the warp events in human history" (TEATD2 5:ix). He does not resist it; he drinks it. "He steals it, just as He stole fire on Molech, and hurls it back." He channels the power through his Custodian bodyguards, the Hetaeron (from the Greek *hetairoi*, the "Companions," the name of Alexander the Great's elite cavalry), using them as extra hands and eyes until they burn out one by one. And as he fills with the warp's power, he becomes the thing everyone feared: "Your Emperor is the Dark King" (TEATD2 7:xliv). Malcador sees it from the Throne: "the Emperor, empowered by the warp, the brightest thing in the galaxy," and "the light that casts the shadow of the Dark King" (TEATD2 6:viii).

**He gives it up.** Oll Persson, his oldest friend, argues with him, speaking through the body of the last surviving Custodian. Loken tells him: "Better to fight daemons as men, than become them" (TEATD2 8:xiv). The Emperor throws the power away. "The power of the Dark King is expelled and scattered, pouring back into the empyrean from whence it came" (TEATD2 8:xvi). The blast restores his companions, healing wounds and even making Oll's rifle new. Then: "Mere mortal now, divinity cast aside, but still a being of perpetual power" (TEATD2 8:xvi). He draws his sword and calls to everyone still loyal across the planet: "*Those who may hear me, join me now.*"

Malcador, alone, sees two things. First, that the Emperor has doomed himself: "The Dark King would have won this day, and then lost everything, ever after." Second, that before going on, "My lord and friend has broken off a part of his soul" (TEATD2 8:xvi): the part that held his hope, loyalty and compassion, set adrift in the warp so it cannot stay his hand. The narration is blunt: "It is no longer a case that he must die. Now, he *will*."

**The death of Sanguinius.** In the Lupercal Court, Sanguinius nearly beats Horus, and offers to make it quick "out of my love for you." "There is no mercy here," Horus answers (TEATD2 8:xvi). Horus stops holding back. The Chaos gods themselves watch from the shadows. Horus breaks Sanguinius with his mace, lifts him in the Talon, and closes it: "The claws close. There is a double crack of spine and neck." Then: "Horus sighs and walks away. Things scurry forward from the shadows to nail the body up" (TEATD2 8:xxiii).

### Volume III: the end and the death

**The duel.** The Emperor has fought his way through the ship to Horus's Court. "When you clash with your father, the whole world shakes" (TEATD3 9:xxi). Horus tells himself he offered his father a throne at his side and was ignored. He believes the Chaos gods have made him something new: "Within Chaos, there can be no heresy" (TEATD3 9:xxi).

They fight with every weapon they have, and each blow moves them through space and time. The Emperor parries by moving them both to Cthonia, the gang-world where Horus was found as a child: "This may be the very day on which you were found" (TEATD3 9:xxi). Horus answers with the Himalazia mountains on Terra; the Emperor with Isstvan V; Horus with Molech. The duel is being fought everywhere at once, from Mohenjo-Daro to Ullanor to Tizca on Prospero.

On Molech Horus seems to kill his father, and the Emperor explodes into ash. But it was only a piece of him: "He merely sacrificed an aspect to end His agony and cover His escape" (TEATD3 9:xxi).

**The chase.** Horus follows a trail of spilled years through human history:
- the burned desolation of Macragge, New Byzantium, the underworld of Calth and the wastes of Tallarn;
- an island off ancient Britain with a wet handprint on a standing stone;
- a longhouse on Davin and a compound on the Moon;
- a limestone cave with "crude handprints" on its walls.

Then he drops into the warp, and the chase runs through the realms listed in reading 4: the Gulf of Lament, the Bastion Stair, the Mists of Unreason, the Blizzard of Forgetting and the Crook of Shadows. The Emperor comes after him in one shape after another: magician, wolf, architect, raven, horseman, lion, avenging son. Horus concludes: "Your father is a master of the aspects. He is, and always has been, an entire arcana. He has outplayed you" (TEATD3 9:xxi).

Horus hides in the Marcher Fortress, raises walls of earth and flame, and waits. Then he decides to face his father as himself, and lets all his defences go. At that moment he realises the Emperor has taken "the aspect of the seducer" and tricked him into dropping his guard.

**The duel of cards.** The fight becomes a game of tarot. Horus has made his own deck by hand, the Empyreal Tarot, "while the Old Four whispered suggestions for the images," and in his realm the cards "can set" outcomes rather than predict them (TEATD3 9:xxi). He plays *The Revenger*, *The Occulted Orphan* and *The Dark King*. His *Dreadful Sagittary* slays the Emperor's *Steadfast Angel* "for a second time," after Sanguinius. Finally he lays *Despoiler* across the Emperor's *Silver Door*, the door opens, and Horus's mace catches the Emperor in the throat and drops him to the deck. The Emperor's last cards lie beside him: *The Guardsman*, *The Throne*, *The Space Marine*, *The Knight of Mandatio*, *The Lantern* and *Revelation*.

**The coronation.** Horus does not kill his father. He wants him to see. He sets the broken Emperor on a throne he has carved and plans to crown him and give him "The Revelation" (TEATD3 10:iv). The daemons protest: "This isn't what was supposed to happen. This isn't what was ordained" (TEATD3 10:iv).

The Emperor revives, drawing a little power from Horus through "that tender, casual touch." He throws spinning **pentacles**, sigils drawn "from His arcanology" (TEATD3 10:v), and Horus destroys all but three. He seizes the Emperor's left arm in the Talon and strips it to the bone, drives a single claw through his father's throat, and nails him to the throne with the three surviving pentacles: "One drives down through His right thigh, the second through His left. The third you ram through His left shoulder" (TEATD3 10:v). "Behold," he cries, "the False Emperor!"

**The three who come.**
- **Caecaltus Dusk**, the last Custodian, stands in the way: "I defy you … The Imperium defies you" (TEATD3 10:vi). He speaks with a voice Horus says is not his, calling Horus "my son." Horus burns him to nothing with the light from the eye on his chest. Only his breastplate survives, with Malcador's sigil on it.
- **Leetu**, meanwhile, climbs the throne and pulls out two of the pentacles, burning his hands. The third, in the shoulder, will not come. Horus finds him, reads "No name … Just a number. LE two" (TEATD3 10:vi), tells him that Erda is dead, and flicks him away across the Court. He lands among the feet of four vast beings. "There are four of them" (TEATD3 10:vi). The text does not name them. It does not need to.
- **Loken**, elsewhere, fights three centaurs with Horus's face in a moonlit water garden and kills them. The dying leader calls him "Oathbreaker!" Loken answers: "My father's truth is no longer that truth" (TEATD3 10:ix). The verse is titled for a card, *The Knight of Mandatio*.

**The last companions.** Oll Persson and John Grammaticus reach the Court by their own road, a path cut through the warp with the athame, and find the Emperor lying broken on the deck. John goes to look for a stone sharp enough to use. They never see each other again. Oll kneels by his oldest friend, presses the athame into the unconscious hand, "I think it might be the only thing you can use" (TEATD3 10:xii), and begs him to get up. In the dust he finds two cards, *The Guardsman* and *The Lantern*: "That's you and me, then" (TEATD3 10:xii). Horus rises. Oll stands between Horus and the Emperor with a lasrifle and fires on full-auto until the Talon kills him. The verse's last line is in italics, like a caption on a holy picture: "*Oll Persson stands his ground against Horus.*" (TEATD3 10:xii).

**The Lantern.** On Terra, the Astronomican, the Emperor's psychic beacon, has been choked by the Death Guard's sorcery. For eight seconds a small psychic "blink" breaks the choke, and the saint Euphrati Keeler and her faithful chant through it: "Speak this with me, as it is spoken to me. The Emperor must live" (TEATD3 10:xv). The beacon blazes. Out in the void, Roboute Guilliman's relief fleet sees by it and turns for the flagship: "If my father still lives, he cannot fight alone" (TEATD3 10:xv). Horus sees the light at his windows and knows the war on two fronts has come.

**The killing.** Horus turns to crown his father and finds the throne empty. The Emperor is behind him with the third pentacle, "gripped like a chakram" (TEATD3 10:viii), and cuts him. He fights on with Caecaltus's spear, and when Horus bends to lift him, he drives Horus's own crown of bloodlight into his face.

Then Horus crushes the Emperor's head into the deck, "his final blasphemy" (TEATD3 10:xvi), while Loken watches in tears. He feels no joy. A voice he takes for Loken's tells him to let go of his power, just for a moment, and feel what he has done: "Be a man and show you are sensible to it" (TEATD3 10:xvi). He lets go. Three things are revealed at once:
- **The whispers.** They are one phrase, spoken by "an entire species": "*The Emperor must live*."
- **The corpse.** It crumbles to ash: "It was just an aspect, another discarded aspect, another empty husk" (TEATD3 10:xvi).
- **Loken.** The Loken who spoke was the Emperor. The real Loken still lies where Horus threw him.

Horus, stripped of his power for that instant, is only Horus. He understands that he was never a god, only "A puppet on their strings," and that the Emperor kept a truth "for thirty thousand years" (TEATD3 10:xvi). His eyes beg his father to end it. The Emperor holds Oll's stone knife, the ancient murder-weapon with its promised eighth death. He hesitates. The Chaos gods rush their power back into Horus. The Emperor speaks: "+I wait for you and I forgive you.+" And "He drives it into your heart" (TEATD3 10:xvi). Loken, watching, sees "the quick mercy-stroke of a Custodian's misericordia" (TEATD3 10:xvii), and the Emperor pours his whole will down the blade. Horus smiles, and it is "the smile Loken remembers from long ago." Then the flesh goes. "And in the end, it's just a man killing his son with a stone" (TEATD3 10:xvii).

**After.** "This is the end and the death. But it is neither the end expected nor the death foretold" (TEATD3 10:xviii).
- **The warp withdraws.** The Chaos gods lose their anchor and fall away, "wailing in torment, dragging the warp with them" (TEATD3 10:xviii). Time begins again. The daemons vanish from the material world, and the traitor armies break and flee.
- **Ahriman.** In a dark archive under the Palace he sets down his tarot deck: "The books have stopped bleeding … There has been a death" (TEATD3 10:xviii).
- **Dorn and Valdor** find the Emperor barely alive, with Loken kneeling beside Horus's body (TEATD3 10:xix). Leetu gathers the scattered cards. *The Throne* lay beside the Emperor, with his blood on it, and Dorn takes it as his father's wish to return there. *Revelation* lay beside Caecaltus's remains. When Dorn wipes the ash from the proconsul's breastplate, Malcador's sigil glows, and at that instant the teleport lock that will carry them home is established.
- **Loken** will not go: "He was Horus Lupercal … And he was my father. I am the only one left who cares" (TEATD3 10:xix). He stays behind to keep vigil, and weeps.

**The Throne.** On Terra, Malcador, still alive only as "the sigil of me," has held the Throne until his friend came home: "Thus is my friend returned to me" (TEATD3 10:xx). Dorn and Valdor carry the Emperor up the steps believing the Throne will save him. Malcador knows it is the other way round, and does not tell them: "He is the throne's only chance" (TEATD3 10:xx). He blesses his friend, "May your death live forever, my friend. There is nothing immortal about this" (TEATD3 10:xx), and is lifted from the seat.

**The story begins.** In the mud outside the Palace, a column of convict gunners trudges home, led by Marshal Agathe. With them walks a woman who calls herself Katerina Moriana, telling the soldiers stories about a man who died well: "His name was Ollanius" (TEATD3 F:i). Agathe asks whether it is a lie. "A good lie is better than a bad truth," Moriana says (TEATD3 F:i).

**The heirs.** On the retreating flagship, the First Captain, Ezekyle Abaddon, lays the Talon of Horus on the strategium table, "so it is clear who owns them now" (TEATD3 F:iv), and orders the shields up. "Ten thousand new years begin here too" (TEATD3 F:iv).

## 5. The rules of the universe

*Series background, unless an address is given.*

### The warp

Everything in this universe rests on the **warp**, also called the **immaterium** or the **empyrean**. It is a second dimension lying alongside ours, made of raw psychic energy. It has no fixed space or time. It is shaped by the emotions of every thinking being that has ever lived: rage, hope, lust, despair and faith all leave currents in it.

Starships cross the galaxy by diving into the warp and coming out again far away. **Psykers**, people born with psychic power, draw on it for their abilities, and every psyker is a door that something on the other side can try to open. That is why the Imperium fears and controls them.

### The Chaos gods

Over the ages, the strongest currents in the warp gathered into four vast intelligences, the **Chaos gods**. The trilogy calls them "the Old Four" and, tellingly, almost never names them.

| God | Born from | Wants | Colour and number in the setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Khorne** | Rage, violence | Blood and skulls; honourable slaughter | Red, eight |
| **Tzeentch** | Hope, ambition, scheming | Change for its own sake; endless plots | Blue, nine |
| **Nurgle** | Despair, disease, endurance | To love and keep all things in rot | Green, seven |
| **Slaanesh** | Excess, pleasure, pride | Every sensation pushed past its limit | Pink and purple, six |

Belief and emotion feed them. The more humanity worships, fears or rages, the stronger they grow. That is the reason behind the Imperial Truth: the Emperor forbade religion because he knew gods were real, and he knew what worship fed.

The **Dark King** is a fifth power that does not yet exist. Some in the setting fear, and some hope, that it might be born out of humanity itself. The trilogy makes it a live threat.

### Daemons and the Neverborn

The gods' creatures are **daemons**, beings of warp-stuff that can enter the real world only where the barrier is thin or a psyker lets them in. The trilogy calls them the **Neverborn**. Their greater servants include daemon princes: mortals raised up by a god, as Fulgrim and Magnus both were.

### Perpetuals and the Cabal

A handful of humans cannot stay dead. When killed, they revive. The books call them **Perpetuals**. Several are central to the trilogy: **Oll Persson**, an ordinary man from the deep past; **John Grammaticus**, a manufactured Perpetual; **Erda**, an ancient woman with her own history with the Emperor; and **Vulkan**, the primarch, whose Perpetual nature the trilogy describes as a curse.

The **Cabal** is a conspiracy of alien races introduced in the Heresy novel *Legion*. It had calculated that if Horus won, humanity would destroy itself and take Chaos with it, so it worked for his victory. Grammaticus was its agent before he broke with it.

### The webway and the Throne

The **webway** is a labyrinth of tunnels outside both real space and the warp, built by an ancient alien race, the aeldari. The Emperor meant to take it over for humanity. The **Golden Throne** was the engine at the heart of that project. After Magnus's breach it became a plug in a wound, and whoever sits on it holds back the daemons in the tunnels beneath the Palace, at the price of being burned alive by the power flowing through them. Only the Emperor can survive it for long.

### Astropaths and the Imperial Tarot

The **Astronomican** is a psychic beacon projected from a mountain on Terra, the Hollow Mountain, by a choir of psykers and fed by the Emperor's own mind. Ships steer by it through the warp; without it they are blind. In the Siege it has been choked by enemy sorcery.

**Astropaths** are psykers who carry messages across space by telepathy. Before they can do it safely, they are soul-bound to the Emperor, and the binding floods their eyes with his power and blinds them.

The **Imperial Tarot**, or Emperor's Tarot, is a deck of psychic divination cards. In the setting the cards are thin psychoactive wafers, and the Emperor himself used a deck. The cards the trilogy names at its climax are *The Guardsman*, *The Throne*, *The Space Marine*, *The Knight of Mandatio*, *The Lantern* and *Revelation* (TEATD3 9:xxi). The trilogy's climax is fought partly as a game of tarot, and every one of those six cards turns out to stand for someone still on the board (Part Two, reading 12).

## 6. Who's who

Descriptions are *series background* unless a verse is cited.

### The father and his household

- **The Emperor of Mankind.** Immortal, the most powerful psychic who has ever lived, and the author of the Imperial Truth. In the trilogy he is almost never given thoughts of his own until the very end. Everyone else sees him from outside, and Malcador, who knew him longest, admits at the last that "perhaps, just like everyone else, I have only ever seen what he has allowed me to see."
- **Malcador the Sigillite.** The Emperor's oldest friend, a mortal psychic thousands of years old, Regent of Terra. He takes the Emperor's place on the Golden Throne in Volume I so that his lord can go and fight, knowing the Throne will burn him to death. He narrates his own sections in the first person.
- **Constantin Valdor.** Captain-General of the Custodes, the Emperor's personal guard. He goes with the Emperor to the flagship.
- **Caecaltus Dusk.** A Custodes proconsul, a senior officer. He has stood guard at the foot of the Throne, and he is chosen to go with the Emperor on the assault (TEATD1 2:xxi).

### The sons

- **Horus Lupercal.** The first-found son and the favourite, made Warmaster, now the champion of all four Chaos gods and swollen with their power. His sections are written entirely in the second person. His weapons are the **Talon**, a huge gauntlet of long blades, and **Worldbreaker**, a mace.
- **Sanguinius.** Primarch of the Blood Angels. He has great white wings and is the most beloved of the sons, the one most like his father. He has foreseen his own death at Horus's hands. He reaches Horus first and is killed at the end of Volume II.
- **Rogal Dorn.** Primarch of the Imperial Fists, the Praetorian of Terra, who has commanded the Palace's defence through the whole siege. Stubborn, unyielding, loyal.
- **Magnus the Red.** Primarch of the Thousand Sons, a one-eyed giant sorcerer, the son who most wanted to know. His warning broke the Emperor's great project. He is now a daemon prince of Tzeentch. As the trilogy opens, Vulkan has just killed him in the webway, "that death won't last" (TEATD1 1:xxiii).
- **Vulkan.** Primarch of the Salamanders, a smith and a protector. He is a Perpetual, so he cannot stay dead. The trilogy calls it a curse. He guards the webway gate beneath the Throne.
- **Fulgrim.** Primarch of the Emperor's Children, fallen to Slaanesh and made a daemon prince. He appears in the trilogy mainly as a gift the Emperor once gave and can take back.
- **Lorgar and Erebus.** Lorgar, primarch of the Word Bearers, and **Erebus**, his First Chaplain. They led the first worship of the Chaos gods, and Erebus arranged Horus's corruption. Erebus is aboard the flagship with Horus.
- **Ezekyle Abaddon.** First Captain of the Sons of Horus, Horus's most loyal officer. In the wider setting he becomes the great villain of the forty-first millennium.

### The mortals and the Perpetuals

- **Garviel Loken.** A captain of the Luna Wolves who stayed loyal when his Legion fell. He survived Isstvan III and has been fighting Horus ever since. He is the moral centre of the whole Heresy series, and he is aboard the flagship at the end: "I still am a captain of the Luna Wolves … it's just that there are no Luna Wolves left" (TEATD2 7:xli).
- **Leetu.** "Leetu is a Space Marine" (TEATD1 1:xvi), in unpainted silver armour, possibly an early prototype of the breed, "his genetic profile unmodified by any primarch's seed" (TEATD1 1:xvi): a Space Marine who is nobody's son. He was the bodyguard of the Perpetual Erda, loaned to John Grammaticus. He ends up nearer the Emperor than anyone at the crisis.
- **Oll Persson.** A Perpetual, and the oldest human in the story: "I was born something over forty thousand years ago, here on Terra" (TEATD1 4:xi). He has been farmer, soldier, scholar, ruler and voyager, and "He was even Warmaster once, the first ever to bear that title" (TEATD1 1:xvi). He knew the Emperor in the deep past and once stabbed him to stop him. He has crossed the galaxy to Terra with a small band of companions, cutting paths through the warp with an ancient stone knife, the athame, meaning to stop the Emperor leading humanity to what he calls "the Triumph of Ruin" (TEATD1 1:xvi).
- **John Grammaticus.** "Pawn of the xenos Cabal, an artificial Perpetual" (TEATD1 1:xvi), who broke with his makers and now travels with Oll.
- **Actae.** A blind prophetess in tattered red, of great and ancient power, travelling with Oll's band. She was once "Cyrene Valantion, the Blessed Lady" (TEATD1 1:xxv), a priestess among Lorgar's people, killed by the Custodes and brought back by sorcery.
- **Kyril Sindermann.** A scholar and former *iterator*, an official preacher of the Imperial Truth. He has lived to see the Truth fail and a religion grow up around the Emperor.
- **"Alpharius."** Not the primarch but a warrior of the Alpha Legion, where every legionary may answer to the name: "We're all Alpharius, John. You know that" (TEATD1 1:xxviii). This one guides Oll's band through secret routes under the Palace, acting under a code word that Actae triggered in his mind.

## 7. How the books are written

Four techniques matter for everything in Part Two.

1. **Horus is "you."** Every Horus section is in the second person: "As He bursts in on you, a brutal iron fist with overwhelming strength, you draw your hand up to meet Him" (TEATD3 9:xxi). The reader is put inside the traitor's head, told what they feel, flattered and goaded. Part Two, reading 6, turns on this.
2. **Numbered verses.** Each chapter is divided into short numbered sections with their own titles, written in Roman numerals: 9:xxi is chapter 9, section xxi, "Heart of neverness." Scripture is laid out this way. It is also why this companion can cite the books the way a commentary cites the Bible.
3. **A dying narrator.** Malcador narrates his sections in the first person, from the Throne, while it burns him away: "Almost all of me is gone now" (TEATD2 5:viii). He sees further into the warp than anyone ever has and can affect nothing.
4. **The Emperor has no inside.** For almost three volumes he is only seen from outside, by his sons, his servants and his enemy. He stays a god, unknowable, until he is a broken body on the floor.

# Part Two — The readings

Each reading has up to four parts:
- **In the story:** the scene it rests on.
- **The reading:** the interpretation itself.
- **Myth and scripture:** the source it draws on, told for someone who has not read it.
- **Correction** (where needed): what an earlier version of the reading got wrong, and what the text actually says.

## 1. The key to all of it: hell is a memory of the warp

**In the story.** In Volume II, Malcador has been on the Golden Throne for what feels like an eternity, burning away. The Throne has destroyed his titles, his body and even his name. Through what little sight he has left, he watches the Emperor fight through the flagship, which has become a landscape of the unleashed warp. Malcador has never seen the warp so wholly unleashed, and it reminds him of something. For centuries, as Regent, he visited a secret collection beneath the Palace: art, poems and prophecies about hell, gathered by the Emperor from every age of human history and preserved by the Sigillites through the Age of Strife. He had always wondered why they were so alike. Now, watching, he understands. The pictures have come to life.

**The reading.** Malcador states the key to the whole trilogy: "I have long believed that the human concept of a 'hell', which has haunted mankind down the ages, and informed the structures of its makeshift religions, is derived from the warp" (TEATD2 5:viii). Chaos has no native geography. It borrows ours, degraded, and every earthly underworld is a partial map of it, sketched by a prophet or a poet who glimpsed the real thing. So when a place in the book reads like Dante or Gilgamesh or Bunyan, the book's own logic says those poets were remembering this place. That is the licence for every other reading in this companion.

It also reverses the normal direction of allusion. Most writers borrow from Dante. Abnett's premise is that Dante borrowed from the warp, and that the Emperor, who collected Dante, knew it.

**Myth and literature: the underworlds the trilogy draws on.** Nearly every culture has imagined a land of the dead, and the trilogy treats all of them as partial memories of one real place. The three it uses most:
- **Dante's *Inferno*** (early 1300s), the first part of the *Divine Comedy*. The poet Virgil guides Dante down through nine concentric circles of Hell, each punishing a sin more severe than the last, down to Satan frozen in ice at the centre of the earth. The sins of appetite are in the upper circles. Past the walls of the city of **Dis**, guarded by fallen angels, are the sins of malice and fraud.
- **The *Epic of Gilgamesh***, the oldest great work of literature, from ancient Mesopotamia. When Gilgamesh's friend Enkidu is dying, he dreams of the underworld, **Irkalla**, the "house of dust," where the dead sit in darkness, clothed like birds in feathers, "where dust is their food and clay their bread."
- **John Bunyan's *The Pilgrim's Progress*** (1678), an allegory in which a man called Christian flees the City of Destruction for the Celestial City. On the way he passes through places named for the trials of the soul: the Slough of Despond, Vanity Fair, the Valley of Humiliation, Doubting Castle.

## 2. The form is revelation

**In the story.** The trilogy is written in short numbered sections. Volume III's great chase (reading 5) runs for more than seven thousand words across one verse, 9:xxi, "Heart of neverness." It passes through dozens of places, from Cthonia, the Himalazia and Molech to Mohenjo-Daro, Tallarn, a longhouse on Davin and a cave full of handprints, then through the warp's realms, one after another, with almost no connecting explanation.

**The reading.** *Apokalypsis* is Greek for "unveiling," and the Book of Revelation is its model. Abnett chose the form, not just the mood. The chapters are divided into numbered verses. The narrators report what they see rather than what happened. The realms arrive without connectives, as the seals and bowls do in Revelation. And the warp is written throughout as a fluid, a sea with tides, trenches and shores, so realms reach a traveller the way flotsam reaches a swimmer. Nobody navigates the chase by choosing a road; the names surface as the current brings them. Revelation's structure and the warp's nature turn out to be the same shape.

The stream also erodes. Every realm takes something from whoever passes: reason, memory, idols, uprightness, names. By the time the Emperor has escaped Horus's labyrinth, he has spent almost everything he took, and the revelation is complete because nothing of the seer is left to veil it. The verse that ends the whole war is itself titled "Revelation" (TEATD3 10:xix), and the last card on the floor, beside the dead Custodian, is *Revelation*.

**Scripture: the Book of Revelation.** Revelation, also called the Apocalypse, is the last book of the Christian Bible. Its author, John, exiled on the Greek island of Patmos, records a series of visions of the end of the world. A scroll sealed with seven seals is opened one seal at a time, each releasing a catastrophe: the four horsemen, earthquakes, stars falling. Seven angels blow seven trumpets and pour out seven bowls of God's wrath. A beast rises from the sea, and a whore rides it. Babylon the great city falls, and at the end a new Jerusalem descends from heaven.

Two things about its form matter here. The visions are not connected by cause and effect; they arrive one after another, joined by "and I saw," as in a dream. And the text is laid out, like all of the Bible, in numbered chapters and verses. The Greek word for the book's form, *apokalypsis*, means "unveiling": something hidden being shown.

## 3. The seer's cost: the hand raised, the tears

**Thesis raised in discussion.** Reading these books, you feel yourself wanting to raise a hand and stop the visions. The more you see, the less you can. It brings tears to the eyes.

**In the story.** At the height of the duel the Emperor comes at Horus as "a brutal iron fist." At the coronation Horus's great gift to his father is to be forced to see: "The Revelation." And at the end, almost everyone weeps.

**The reading.** The gesture is in the book: "As He bursts in on you, a brutal iron fist with overwhelming strength, you draw your hand up to meet Him" (TEATD3 9:xxi). The last weapon Horus holds is seeing itself: "The Revelation. You can share your gnosis with Him" (TEATD3 10:iv). He means to make his father see the truths of Chaos forever, which is torture.

Then the tears, and the book is careful about whose:
- Horus hears Loken weeping: "You understand Loken's grief" (TEATD3 10:xvi).
- Dorn, kneeling by the dying Emperor, sees the Custodes weep without a sound: Valdor's silent tears, "and somehow far worse" (TEATD3 10:xix).
- Malcador, dying: "My heart breaks to see the tears in your eyes" (TEATD3 10:xx).
- Loken stays behind on the dying flagship with his father's body, and only "Now there is no one left to see, he weeps" (TEATD3 10:xix).
- The Sons of Horus fleeing Terra at the very end are "unaware that they are weeping" (TEATD3 F:iv).

That last group is the reader, who reaches the end and finds they are already crying. The Emperor on the Throne is the one who was never allowed to raise the hand: ten thousand years of not being permitted to stop seeing.

**Scripture: what seeing costs the prophets.** The Hebrew Bible is unusually honest that seeing God damages the one who sees.
- **Moses** asks to see God's glory. God answers, "Thou canst not see my face: for there shall no man see me, and live," and puts him in a cleft of the rock and covers him with his hand while he passes, so that Moses sees only his back (Exodus 33:18–23).
- **Isaiah** sees the Lord on a throne, surrounded by seraphim, and cries: "Woe is me! for I am undone … for mine eyes have seen the King, the Lord of hosts" (Isaiah 6:5).
- **Daniel**, after his great vision by the river Hiddekel: "there remained no strength in me: for my comeliness was turned in me into corruption" (Daniel 10:8).
- **John of Patmos**, when the vision of Christ first appears to him: "I fell at his feet as dead" (Revelation 1:17).

## 4. The realms, and what each one means

**In the story.** By Volume II the warp has swallowed the flagship, and the flagship has fused with the Palace and with a warp-city called the Inevitable City. In Volume III the duel moves through dozens of named realms. Every one is listed in the Appendix, with where it first appears, what the book says about it, and its mythic source.

**The reading.** Abnett never assigns a realm to a Chaos god by name. The Bastion Stair lies "in the realm of the red," which a reader of the setting knows is Khorne's colour; the Planes of Excess are Slaanesh's; the Long Woe, with its fever-meadows, is Nurgle's garden. But the text only ever names places by what they do to a traveller. The mythic sources do the work the god-names would have done. The four powers don't own the geography. The geography is older than they are, and it is ours.

Some of the realms, and what they carry:

- **The frame.** *Regno Kao*, the Realm of Chaos, is the paper the other realms are drawn on: "nothing retains the semblance of sense." *Neverness* is not a place but a condition with no clock. Horus has made his Court into "a frozen infinite without laws" (TEATD2 5:ix), and time only starts again when he dies.
- **Three underworld cities, one per volume, from three traditions.**
  - The **Inevitable City** is a city made of every city, stolen streets bored through each other. It is Dante's Dis, Babylon the Great and Milton's Pandemonium, and its Latin name, *Urbs Ineluctabilis*, means the thing you cannot struggle out of.
  - The **City of Dust** is Irkalla: an empty city of memory, left behind wherever the Emperor has passed.
  - **Somnopolis**, the Library of Lost and Mislaid Dreams, is Borges's infinite library shelved in Ovid's Cave of Sleep, and it is destroyed in the collapse: an archive of everything unmade, burned like Alexandria.
- **The crossings.**
  - The **Gulf of Lament** is crossed on a bridge of living flesh over a bottomless pit: the Chinvat bridge of judgment. The bridge is the inhabitant.
  - The **Bastion Stair** is Jacob's ladder made of brass; the steps scream, because the souls are the steps.
  - The **Hall of Leng** is a "thin place," Jacob's Bethel, "the gate of heaven," over Lovecraft's plateau.
- **The chase is a pilgrimage run backwards.** In Bunyan the pilgrim flees toward salvation through places named for sins. Here the fleeing pilgrim is Horus and the pursuer is God. The **Blizzard of Forgetting** costs a learned name at every step. The **Desert of Gods**, "where no idol is permitted to stand," pours away like an hourglass. The **Crook of Shadows** is "that dingy, slanting subdimension where nothing is upright and everything is corners."
- **The coordinates are a Passion sequence with the roles scrambled.** The *Twelfth Intersection* is the twelve tribes. The *Sixty-sixth Oblique*, under a skull-coloured moon, is Golgotha, "the place of a skull," one digit short of the number of the beast: exactly what Horus is, a not-quite-Antichrist. The *Eightieth Conjunction* is a lifetime. The *Forsaken Angle*, the last place the Emperor can reach before he is caught, is Psalm 22.
- **The Lupercal Court**, Horus's own throne room, turns Dante's universe inside out. Horus sees it as nested shells: "Your Court, which surrounds your flagship, which surrounds the neverness, which surrounds the Inevitable City" (TEATD3 10:viii), and outward to the whole galaxy and the warp. Dante's Satan is frozen at the bottom of the universe with every heaven above him. Horus sits at the centre with everything, including the warp, as a shell around him.

**Myth and literature: the sources of the realms.**
- **Hesiod's *Theogony*** (Greek, about 700 BC) says that "first of all Chaos came into being": a yawning void before any form. **Ovid**, opening the *Metamorphoses*, describes it as "a rude and undigested mass."
- **Dis** is Dante's walled city of lower Hell. **Pandemonium**, "all the demons," is the capital the fallen angels build in Milton's *Paradise Lost*. **Babylon the Great** is the city of sin in Revelation.
- **Irkalla** is the Mesopotamian house of dust, from *Gilgamesh*.
- **Jorge Luis Borges**, "The Library of Babel" (1941), imagines the universe as an infinite library of every possible book, almost all of them nonsense. **Ovid's Cave of Sleep** (*Metamorphoses* XI) is the home of the god of sleep and his thousand sons, the dreams. **The Library of Alexandria** is the great library of antiquity whose burning became a symbol of lost knowledge.
- **The Chinvat Bridge** is the Zoroastrian bridge the dead must cross to judgment. It is broad for the righteous and turns on its edge, narrow as a blade, for the wicked, who fall into hell. The Islamic *Sirat* is a similar bridge over hell.
- **Jacob's ladder** comes from Genesis 28. The patriarch Jacob, asleep on a stone, dreams of a ladder reaching from earth to heaven with angels going up and down on it. He wakes and names the place **Bethel**: "this is none other but the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven."
- **The psychomachia**, "battle for the soul," is the Latin poem of Prudentius (about AD 400), in which virtues and vices fight as armoured women. It is the ancestor of every allegory in which places and enemies are named after sins. Bunyan is its most famous descendant.
- **The Passion** is the Christian account of Christ's suffering and death. **Golgotha**, "the place of a skull," is the hill of the crucifixion. On the cross Christ speaks the opening of **Psalm 22**: "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" In Revelation 13 the number of the beast is **666**. **Psalm 90** measures a life as "threescore years and ten," or "fourscore" for the strong.
- **The Lupercal** was a cave at the foot of the Palatine Hill in Rome. Legend says a she-wolf suckled the abandoned twins Romulus and Remus there, and Romulus went on to found the city.
- **Dante's universe** has the earth at the centre and Satan at the bottom of it. Around the earth turn the spheres of the heavens, the outermost of which, the *primum mobile* or "first mover," turns all the others.

## 5. The wolf who wears winter

**In the story.** In Volume III, Horus kills what he thinks is his father on Molech. It is only an aspect the Emperor has sacrificed to escape. Horus follows the trail of spilled years and is ambushed on the Moon, in a cave, in the warp. He weaves a labyrinth in the Crook of Shadows to trap his father, and the Emperor gets out of it. Horus watches the pursuit come after him in one shape after another.

**The reading.** Of all those shapes, the wolf gets the most memorable line: "His sword is an executioner's axe, His canines long, and He drags winter behind Him like a pelt" (TEATD3 9:xxi). The winter here is not weather. It is something a predator wears and trails behind it.

The joke the chase is built on is savage. Horus's surname is *Lupercal*, from the she-wolf's cave. His Legion were the **Luna Wolves**. His cloak, in Volume I, is the pelt of "a lunar wolf for a true Luna Wolf" (TEATD1 1:xxix), taken on the moon of Davin, where he fell. The Emperor picks up his son's own animal, wears it better, and runs him down with it, while Horus, in flight, narrates his rout to himself as tactics.

And the wolf is only one card in the deck. "He has a dynasty of aspects to draw upon, a bloodline of faces and meanings and talents" (TEATD3 9:xxi). Across the chase he is:
- a bloodstained magician with a baleful eye;
- the wolf;
- the great architect, reading the joins of Horus's labyrinth;
- an obliterator;
- a swift and clever raven;
- a steppe horseman;
- a lion hunting on the shores of the Sea of Souls;
- an avenging son;
- the seducer.

Horus's verdict: "He is, and always has been, an entire arcana" (TEATD3 9:xxi).

**Myth: the tarot.** The tarot is a deck of cards, first used for games in Renaissance Italy and later for divination. Its twenty-two trump cards, the **major arcana**, depict archetypes: the Magician, the Emperor, the Lovers, the Tower, the Hanged Man. The word *arcana* means "secrets." To call someone "an entire arcana" is to call him every archetype at once.

## 6. The voice in the second person

**In the story.** Three moments.
- **The Marcher Fortress** (TEATD3 9:xxi). Horus hides in a fortress watching the Planes of Excess. He takes on "the aspect of terror," raises walls of earth and flame, hangs plague-sigils, and nails "a gladiator's agony" into his own heart for rage. Then a thought comes to him: face your father as yourself. He lets every defence go, and the Emperor walks in. Horus realises too late that his father had taken "the aspect of the seducer."
- **The coronation** (TEATD3 10:iv–vi). With his father nailed to a throne, Horus turns aside from killing him, to the fury of the watching daemons: "This isn't what was supposed to happen. This isn't what was ordained" (TEATD3 10:iv). Then Caecaltus Dusk stands in his way and says "You have been tricked, my son." Horus answers: "The voice you speak with is not yours" (TEATD3 10:vi).
- **The last moment** (TEATD3 10:xvi). Horus has crushed his father's head. A voice he takes for Loken's tells him to let go of his power and feel what he has done. He does, and discovers that "Loken" was the Emperor.

**Thesis raised in discussion.** The Emperor used the beguiling aspect he had once given to his son Fulgrim, and impersonated or silenced one of the voices in Horus's head and put his own there instead, more than once in the battle. The reader is never told this outright.

**The reading.** The book supports every part of it, and Volume III ends up showing it in plain sight.
- **The aspects can be called back.** In the chase the Emperor "is drawing on all His gifts, many of them gifts He passed on to His sons" (TEATD3 9:xxi). Horus later catalogues his brothers as "all of them too much like Him," with "Fulgrim too in love with his own glory" (TEATD3 10:xvi). The seducer at the Marcher Fortress is Fulgrim's inheritance played back against the brother who corrupted him.
- **Horus has two channels, and hears only one.** He can hear the whispers and talks back to them: "You are always whispering … Whisper, whisper. It's annoying" (TEATD3 10:iv). The second-person voice that narrates him, "you," he never once answers, because he takes it for his own thoughts. It flatters him, tells him what he wants, and calls it his choice. At the moment he could kill his father it says: "Well, perhaps. It's your choice. You are your own master" (TEATD3 10:iv).
- **The Emperor speaks through others.** Through Caecaltus he says "my son," and Horus hears it (TEATD3 10:vi). At the last he takes Loken's face and voice, the one son Horus still half loves, and uses them to talk Horus out of his power.
- **The whispers were not the daemons.** When Horus lets go, the whispers he has heard "since Maloghurst first woke you" turn out to be one phrase, "uttered in unison by a million voices. Two million. An entire species" (TEATD3 10:xvi): "*The Emperor must live*." It is humanity praying.

Every time, the bait is the same: autonomy. Be your own master; face him as yourself; be a man and feel it. And every time, Horus sets down the winning move to prove he chose freely. The trilogy spent three volumes putting the reader inside Horus's head as "you," and the last trick is that the reader was listening to the same voices he was.

**Series background: Fulgrim and the gifts of the sons.** The primarchs were not only soldiers; each embodied one side of their father. Fulgrim, primarch of the Emperor's Children, was the perfectionist and the aesthete: the son of beauty, charm and pride. In the novel *Fulgrim* he takes up a daemon-haunted sword, is possessed, and kills his brother Ferrus Manus. He becomes a daemon prince of Slaanesh, the god of excess. The seducer is his gift from his father, turned to the enemy's use.

**Correction.** The earlier reading treated the Emperor's use of Horus's inner voice as inference, a shape "never stated." Volume III 10:xvi states the Loken impersonation outright. It also names the whispers as the prayer of humanity. The thesis was more right than the first reading knew.

## 7. The sovereign trickster: Odin, Bolverk, Varuna

**Thesis raised in discussion.** The Emperor is the greatest trickster in fiction, by implication and by outright deed. He is almost certainly Odin, or the godhead behind Woden and Odin, or even the source that gave them their aspects, out of a prehistoric Indo-European pantheon that none of us truly remembers.

**In the story.**
- **The unknown face.** Malcador, who served the Emperor for thousands of years, knows he has never seen his real face (TEATD1 1:v).
- **The first scene.** The trilogy opens with the young Emperor in a cave, watching a painter put images of spear-hunters and antelopes on the wall: "painting tomorrow."
- **The duel's route.** The chase passes through the prehistoric cave with its handprints, a standing stone in ancient Britain, a longhouse, and Molech, where, the series suggests, the Emperor once made a bargain with the powers of the warp and broke it.

**The reading.** Malcador is the only qualified witness: "I have known him by as many names as he has worn masks, and by as many faces" (TEATD1 1:v). The chase reads as an Odin catalogue: the raven, the wolf, the winter, the horseman, the seducer. It ends in a duel of cards, a wisdom contest, the one game Odin always wins and always wins dirty.

Molech is the strongest single piece. The book calls it "the birthplace of all deceit," and Horus sees there "the faint glimmer of the gate through which He once passed, to make promises He would break, and to steal a fire that He would covet for Himself" (TEATD3 9:xxi). He is "old gods antagonised by a solemn pact dishonoured" (TEATD3 9:xxi). In Volume II the Emperor "steals it, just as He stole fire on Molech, and hurls it back" (TEATD2 5:ix). Everyone reads Prometheus there, and they are not wrong. But the closer fit is Odin as Bölverkr, who works a season for the giants under a false name, seduces the guardian, drinks the mead of poetry and flies away with the bargain broken.

The sharpening: he is not a trickster. Loki, Hermes and Coyote are comic, hungry and subordinate, and something above them eventually punishes them. The Emperor has not one joke in three volumes. He is **the sovereign who rules by trickster means**, and there is nothing above him to hand down the reckoning. That is Dumézil's darker sovereign, Varuna, the binder, next to the lawful Mitra. The pair is marked by mutilation, Tyr's lost hand and Odin's given eye. Abnett ends his Emperor with an arm stripped to the bone, "one remaining eye" (TEATD3 10:xvi), and his head broken.

Turn Malcador's key (reading 1) one notch further. If hell is humanity's memory of the warp, the gods may be humanity's memory of him. Nobody remembers the origin, and the reason may be that the origin spent the whole of history making sure of it, then built an Imperium on the premise that none of it had happened.

**Myth: Odin, the god of many names.** In Norse mythology **Odin** is the chief of the gods, and the strangest of them. He is a god of war, wisdom, poetry, magic and the dead, and he goes about the world in disguise under dozens of names. The stories told about him in the *Poetic Edda* and in Snorri Sturluson's *Prose Edda* (13th century) show the shapes the trilogy's Emperor takes:
- **His ravens, Huginn and Muninn**, "Thought" and "Memory," fly over the world each day and come back to tell him what they saw.
- **His wolves, Geri and Freki**, "the Ravenous" and "the Greedy," eat the food from his table.
- **His horse, Sleipnir**, has eight legs and carries him between worlds.
- **His high seat, Hliðskjálf**, lets him look out over every world at once.
- **On the World Tree.** In the poem *Hávamál*, Odin hangs himself on the world-tree Yggdrasil for nine nights, wounded by a spear, "given to Odin, myself to myself," and learns the runes at the end of it.
- **The mead of poetry.** A magical mead made anyone who drank it a poet and a sage. It was kept by the giant Suttungr inside the mountain Hnitbjörg, guarded by his daughter Gunnlöð. Odin, calling himself **Bölverkr**, "evil-doer," worked a summer for Suttungr's brother. He bored a hole into the mountain, went in as a snake, and lay with Gunnlöð for three nights in return for three drinks of the mead. He drank all of it and flew out as an eagle, leaving the giants robbed and the bargain broken.
- **The wisdom contest.** In the poem *Vafþrúðnismál*, Odin in disguise matches wits with the wisest of the giants, the loser to forfeit his head. He wins by asking what he himself whispered into the ear of his dead son Balder on the funeral pyre, a question only Odin can answer.
- **Promises he breaks.** Odin grants victory to his heroes and then withdraws it. The warriors he favours die in battle so that he can gather them in Valhalla for the last war.

**Myth: the tricksters and the fire-thief.** A *trickster* is a figure in myth who breaks rules and crosses boundaries by cunning: **Loki** in the Norse myths, **Hermes** in the Greek, **Anansi** the spider in West African and Caribbean stories, **Coyote** in many Native American traditions. Tricksters are usually comic, greedy and lower in rank than the gods they fool, and they are usually punished. **Prometheus**, in Greek myth, was the Titan who stole fire from the gods and gave it to humanity. Zeus chained him to a rock where an eagle ate his liver every day.

**Scholarship: Dumézil and the two faces of sovereignty.** Georges Dumézil (1898–1986) was a French scholar who compared the myths of peoples descended from the ancient Indo-Europeans, from India to Ireland. He argued they shared a pattern. Sovereignty, the power to rule, was split between two gods. One is **Mitra**, the god of the contract: lawful, bright, trustworthy. The other is **Varuna**, the terrible sovereign who binds his victims with a noose and rules by *maya*, a magic that cannot be told apart from deceit. Dumézil found the pair in Rome, in Romulus the violent founder and Numa the lawgiver, and in the Norse gods **Tyr**, the god of oaths, and **Odin**. He also noticed that the pair is marked by mutilation: **Tyr is one-handed and Odin one-eyed**.

## 8. The Talon as Fenrir's jaws, and the knife of Cain

**Thesis raised in discussion.** Horus's weapon, the **Talon**, a gauntlet of long blades, reads as the jaws of the wolf Fenrir.

**In the story.** When the Emperor stumbles out of the warp at the end of the chase, weakened, Horus catches his arm in the Talon. Horus binds his father with bloodlight and nails him to a throne. Twice, Horus believes he has killed the Emperor, and twice the body turns out to be a husk. At the end, Oll's stone knife goes into Horus's heart.

**The reading.** The text uses the word "bites": "Your Talon seizes Him by the left arm," and "the Talon bites, cracking auramite and drawing blood" (TEATD3 10:v), and then strips the arm to the bone. The myth's reason for the bite matters: Fenrir bit because the gods lied about the binding. That is exactly Horus's grievance about Molech, a pact dishonoured.

- **The fetter goes on the god.** Horus draws bloodlight into "a wire of light, like a red thread" (TEATD3 10:viii), winds it into a serpent loop biting its own tail, and knots it into a crown for his father. That is the magical cord Gleipnir, closed into a ring like the world-serpent.
- **The wolf's fate goes to the god.** In the myth, Fenrir lies bound until the end of the world. In this setting, the Emperor is the one bound to a seat for ten thousand years.
- **The wolf swallows only a husk.** At Ragnarök Fenrir swallows Odin. Horus "kills" his father twice, on Molech and on the deck of his Court, and both times the body turns to ash: "It was just an aspect, another discarded aspect, another empty husk" (TEATD3 10:xvi). The god survives his own Ragnarök by leaving a shape in the wolf's mouth.
- **The avenger strikes the heart.** The chase has already named him, "the fearless certainty of an avenging son" (TEATD3 9:xxi). In the older poem, the *Völuspá*, Vidar kills the wolf with a sword to the heart, and that is how Horus dies: "He drives it into your heart" (TEATD3 10:xvi). One body plays three gods. The Emperor loses the arm as Tyr loses his hand, falls to the wolf as Odin does, and kills it as Vidar does.

**The knife.** The weapon that ends the Heresy has a named history, and the book gives it in Volume I. Oll's athame is "the instrument of the original murder, splashed with the blood of Abel, the executioner of Gog" (TEATD1 1:xvi). "It has killed seven times. It's been promised an eighth death" (TEATD1 1:xvi). In Volume III Oll presses it into the Emperor's unconscious hand, "I think it might be the only thing you can use" (TEATD3 10:xii), and goes to die. When the blow falls, the book says what the eighth death is: "from the original killing that made it, and stained it with the shadow of all murder, to this, the eighth death that it was promised" (TEATD3 10:xvii).

So the last act of the war is a brother-killing weapon used to kill a son, the first murder closing the circle on the last. The narration refuses every grander frame: "And in the end, it's just a man killing his son with a stone" (TEATD3 10:xvii). The stroke is a mercy-stroke. Loken sees "the quick mercy-stroke of a Custodian's misericordia" (TEATD3 10:xvii), the thin dagger a knight used to finish a mortally wounded man. Horus had begged for it with his eyes: "*End this. End it now, if you can*" (TEATD3 10:xvi). Before it lands, the Emperor speaks to him for the only time in the duel: "+I wait for you and I forgive you.+" (TEATD3 10:xvi). And Horus dies with "the smile Loken remembers from long ago" (TEATD3 10:xvii).

**Myth: the binding of Fenrir and the end of the world.** **Fenrir** was a wolf, a son of the trickster Loki, and he grew so large and strong that the gods feared him. Twice they bound him with iron chains, and twice he broke them. Then the dwarves made **Gleipnir**, a fetter as smooth and soft as a silk ribbon, forged from six things that do not exist: the sound of a cat's footfall, the beard of a woman, the roots of a mountain, the sinews of a bear, the breath of a fish, and the spittle of a bird.

Fenrir suspected a trick and agreed to be bound only if one of the gods would put a hand in his mouth as a pledge that it was a game. Only **Tyr** was brave enough. The gods bound him, the ribbon held, and Fenrir bit off Tyr's hand. Fenrir lies bound until the end of the world. The gods kept faith with nothing; the only honest act in the story is Tyr's hand, given knowing it would be lost.

At **Ragnarök**, the end of the world, Fenrir breaks free and swallows Odin whole. Odin's silent son **Vidar** avenges him. In the poem *Völuspá*, Vidar thrusts his sword into the wolf's heart. In Snorri's *Gylfaginning*, he sets one foot, in a shoe made of every scrap of leather ever cut from shoes, on the wolf's lower jaw and tears the jaws apart. Fenrir's sibling **Jörmungandr** is the World Serpent, so long that it circles the whole earth and grips its own tail.

**Scripture and legend: the first murder, Gog, and the mercy-stroke.**
- **Cain and Abel** (Genesis 4). Adam and Eve's first two sons bring offerings to God. God accepts the shepherd Abel's and not the farmer Cain's, and Cain kills his brother in the field. "The voice of thy brother's blood crieth unto me from the ground." Cain is marked and sent away to wander. In Christian and Jewish tradition this is the first murder, the root of all killing after it. The Bible does not say what Cain used. Medieval art often gives him a jawbone or a stone.
- **Gog** is a name out of the Bible's end-times. In Ezekiel 38–39 "Gog, of the land of Magog" leads the nations against Israel in the last days and is destroyed. In Revelation 20, "Gog and Magog" are the nations Satan gathers for the final battle. In British legend, **Gogmagog** was the last of the giants of Albion, thrown from a cliff by the Trojan hero Corineus, a story told by Geoffrey of Monmouth. The athame is "the executioner of Gog."
- **The round table.** The athame has also "lain on the painted top of a great round table" (TEATD1 1:xvi): King Arthur's.
- **The misericordia**, "mercy" in Latin, was the long thin dagger of a medieval knight, used to give the death-stroke through the gaps in armour to a man too badly wounded to live.

**Correction.** An earlier version of this reading had the Emperor kill Horus with the third pentacle. He does not. The pentacle cuts Horus and then shatters (TEATD3 10:viii). The death-blow is the athame, into the heart, which matches the Vidar of the *Völuspá* more closely than the reading first claimed.

## 9. Magnus is not Mimir; Malcador is

**Thesis raised in discussion.** Magnus had been compared to Mimir, the wise severed head. He is not Mimir, or only as an inversion. He is a wisdom-seeker in the line of Solomon and Odin, made an arrogant fool by the Emperor's splitting of his aspects among his sons. Clever and powerful, and a complete fool.

**In the story.** Early in Volume I, Malcador sees Vulkan deep in the webway, having just killed Magnus, trudging home "to take his place at the gate beneath the Throne" (TEATD1 1:xxiii). Later, Malcador reveals the Throne had once been meant for Magnus.

**Series background.** Magnus the Red, primarch of the Thousand Sons, was a sorcerer, and the son who most wanted to know. When he learned by sorcery that Horus had turned, he sent a warning to his father across the warp. The spell tore through the webway project beneath the Palace and let daemons into it. The Emperor was forced onto the Throne to hold the breach. The Space Wolves were sent to bring Magnus to judgment, and they burned his homeworld, Prospero. Magnus gave himself to Tzeentch to save what was left of his Legion.

**The reading.** The inversion is nearly word for word. Mimir's head is preserved, kept close and consulted. Magnus "can't really die," but Vulkan "has vanquished him and banished his deathless corpse into the outer darkness" (TEATD1 1:xxiii). It is a deathless head nobody keeps, thrown to the one place nothing comes back from.

The splitting is stated: Sanguinius "has inherited, like damned Magnus, his father's more esoteric aspect" (TEATD1 2:ix). Magnus is the Odin who trades an eye for sight, broken off from the Odin who rules. Odin pays and learns he will still lose at the end. Magnus pays and believes he has bought an exemption. That is Oedipus, who solves the Sphinx's riddle and cannot see the truth in his own house.

And the Throne was his: "the day we realised that Magnus was no longer a viable candidate" (TEATD1 2:xvi). His conceit cost him the one role he was made for, and an old man burned in his place. He is also the fool the Emperor made: a father who hid everything, and a son who could not bear not to know.

**Who is Mimir, then?** Malcador. He is the counsellor burned down to nothing but a head's worth of mind, "Almost all of me is gone now" (TEATD2 5:viii), and his wisdom still works at the end of the world. His sigil is on the third nail that frees the Emperor (reading 12), and on the breastplate that brings the Emperor home. What survives of him at the last is only "the sigil of me" (TEATD3 10:xx). Dorn, who distrusts the tarot, admits that Malcador "showed me things that have since come true" (TEATD3 10:xix). Odin goes to Mimir's head as the world ends. Dorn goes to Malcador's mark.

**Myth: Mimir, Oedipus and Solomon.**
- **Mimir** was the wisest being in Norse myth, guardian of a well of wisdom under the roots of the world-tree. Odin gave one of his eyes to drink from it. In the *Ynglinga saga*, after a war between two families of gods, Mimir is sent as a hostage to the Vanir, who cut off his head and send it back. Odin preserves the head with herbs, sings charms over it so that it can speak, and consults it for secret knowledge. In *Völuspá*, as the end of the world begins, Odin goes to speak with Mimir's head.
- **Oedipus**, in Sophocles's *Oedipus the King*, is the cleverest man in Thebes. He answered the riddle of the Sphinx and became king. He sets out to find the killer of the old king and learns that it was himself, and that the queen he married is his mother. He blinds himself. He could see everything except the truth in his own house.
- **Solomon**, king of Israel, asked God for wisdom rather than riches and was given both. The Bible's wisdom books are traditionally credited to him. In old age, it says, "his wives turned away his heart after other gods" (1 Kings 11:4). **Proverbs 26:12**: "Seest thou a man wise in his own conceit? there is more hope of a fool than of him."

## 10. Ahriman and the garden of dust

**Thesis raised in discussion.** Ahriman is the one who actually tried to fix the Thousand Sons. And because of the corruption Tzeentch planted in Magnus's gene-seed, nothing wholesome will ever grow from their garden.

**Series background.** The Thousand Sons suffered the **flesh-change**, uncontrolled mutation born of their psychic gifts. Magnus had bargained with Tzeentch, the god of change, to hold it back. **Azhek Ahriman** was the Legion's Chief Librarian, its greatest sorcerer after Magnus himself. In the later story of the setting, after the Heresy, he works a great spell in secret, the **Rubric**, to cure the flesh-change for good. It turns most of the Legion into dust sealed inside their armour, and Magnus exiles him.

**In the story.** Ahriman is in the trilogy only briefly. In the last volume he sits in a dark archive under the Palace, laying out a tarot deck, as the warp withdraws. The pages around him have been bleeding: "The books have stopped bleeding … There has been a death" (TEATD3 10:xviii).

**The reading.** The Gospel states the law of the garden: "a corrupt tree cannot bring forth good fruit." The Sons' sin was forbidden knowledge. The curse on Eden's fruit is "unto dust shalt thou return," and the Rubric produced exactly that: armour with dust inside. The disease is change, and every remedy the Sons can reach comes from the god of change. The only water in that garden is the poisoned spring.

Ahriman also repeated his master's method: a secret rite, against his lord's will, to rewrite a fate. The one son who truly tried did it the family way, which is the corrupt root. Abnett gives him one image in the finale: his tarot cards "discolour, like fallen leaves as the turn of winter blackens gold and red" (TEATD3 10:xviii). The leaves fall, and there is no spring in the garden.

**Scripture: the garden and the fruit.** In **Genesis 2–3**, God places Adam and Eve in the garden of Eden and forbids them one tree, the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. The serpent persuades Eve to eat, she gives the fruit to Adam, and they are driven out. God's sentence on Adam ends: "for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return" (Genesis 3:19). Jesus, in the Sermon on the Mount, gives the rule for telling true teachers from false: "A good tree cannot bring forth evil fruit, neither can a corrupt tree bring forth good fruit … by their fruits ye shall know them" (Matthew 7:18–20).

## 11. Covenant, curse, and the counters built in

**Thesis raised in discussion.** The punishments of Magnus and the Thousand Sons for breaking faith are literally biblical, and perhaps the Emperor built them in: counters to moves he expected the enemy to make. The god of trickery and change got his hooks into the son who was meant for the Throne, and for their perfidy the Sons turned to dust and their father went to the outer dark.

**In the story.** Volume I reveals the Emperor's succession plan in two lines. "The contingency's name was Magnus" (TEATD1 2:xvi). When Magnus failed, Malcador became the reserve and prepared for decades. In Volume III, Malcador is no longer sure the plan was ever his friend's final one: "it was a contingency … He had me believe" (TEATD3 10:xx).

**The reading.** There are two biblical mechanisms, and they are worth keeping apart.

- **The curse inside the covenant.** In Deuteronomy the curses are written into the bond itself. Break faith and they "shall come upon thee, and overtake thee." Nobody has to step in. The Rubric is the Sons' own pit.
- **The sentence by the master's loyal hand.** In the parable of the talents, a servant entrusted with the master's wealth hands it back with an accusation: "I knew thee that thou art an hard man." He is cast "into outer darkness." Magnus, entrusted with his father's gift, makes the same complaint and ends where that servant ends, put there by the loyal son Vulkan.

The redundancy is visible even where traps are not. "Candidate" implies a succession. Sanguinius carries the same aspect as Magnus. Malcador sits when Magnus fails, and the Emperor sits when Malcador burns. The trilogy is full of this kind of layered reserve: the "Unspoken Sanction" behind Malcador (TEATD2 6:vii), the teleport lock that waits on a finger-drawn sigil, and the Astronomican's light that summons Guilliman's fleet. Horus himself sees it at the end: "How many plans did He make, how many gambits did He set in motion, all running together in the hope that one might succeed?" (TEATD3 10:xv). No book says the Emperor wired punishment into his sons. The structure is there; the ambiguity is his.

**Scripture: covenant, curse and the parable of the talents.**
- **The covenant curses.** In **Deuteronomy 28**, Moses sets out the terms of Israel's covenant with God: blessings if the people keep faith, and a long list of curses if they break it. "All these curses shall come upon thee, and overtake thee." The punishment is written into the bond itself; no one has to decide to inflict it.
- **The pit.** "He made a pit, and digged it, and is fallen into the ditch which he made" (Psalm 7:15).
- **The parable of the talents** (Matthew 25:14–30). A master going on a journey leaves his wealth with three servants: five talents to one, two to another, one to the third. The first two trade and double the money. The third buries his talent in the ground and, when the master returns, hands it back with an accusation: "Lord, I knew thee that thou art an hard man, reaping where thou hast not sown." The master takes the talent from him: "unto every one that hath shall be given … but from him that hath not shall be taken away even that which he hath. And cast ye the unprofitable servant into outer darkness: there shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth."
- **The heirs cast out.** "The children of the kingdom shall be cast out into outer darkness" (Matthew 8:12). In the Gospels the "outer darkness" is the place outside the lit banquet hall: the ones thrown there are the ones who were invited.
- **Odin's preparation.** Knowing Ragnarök will come, Odin gathers the best of the warriors who die in battle, the *einherjar*, into Valhalla, to fight for him on the last day. He cannot stop the end, so he builds the reserves he will need at it.

## 12. Every card I turn over, I win

**Thesis raised in discussion.** Splitting and mixing his aspects across sons and servants is how the Emperor counters the gods' moves. Every card he turns over wins. He may lose too, but he is winning.

**In the story.** The climax of the duel is fought with tarot. Horus's own hand-made deck can "set" outcomes, and he plays it perfectly. The Emperor's cards burn in his hands, and he goes down. Six are left scattered beside him: *The Guardsman*, *The Throne*, *The Space Marine*, *The Knight of Mandatio*, *The Lantern* and *Revelation*. "A weak reading, of ill fortune, and in disarray" (TEATD3 9:xxi). Horus, reading them, sees a lost hand.

**The reading.** Each of those six cards is a person or a thing still on the board, and every one of them plays. The book titles the verses after them.

| Card | Who plays it | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| ***The Space Marine*** | **Leetu**, the Space Marine who is nobody's son | "A stray card … *The Space Marine*" falls as he creeps in (TEATD3 10:iv). He climbs the throne and pulls out two of the three nails with his bare hands. |

| ***The Guardsman*** | **Oll Persson** | Oll finds this card and *The Lantern* in the dust beside the Emperor. "That's you and me, then," he says. "You and me" (TEATD3 10:xii). He pockets the Guardsman, lays the Lantern on the Emperor's chest, and then stands between Horus and his old friend with a lasrifle until the Talon kills him. Later, Leetu finds the card "torn in two" (TEATD3 10:xix). |
| ***The Lantern*** | **The Emperor and the Astronomican** | Oll lays it on the Emperor as his own card. In the next verse, titled for it, a psychic "blink" lets the Astronomican, the Emperor's beacon, shine through for the first time. Guilliman's fleet sees by it and comes: "As we are illuminated, so we will illuminate" (TEATD3 10:xv). Horus notices the card beside the body and laughs at it: "Most amusing. | ***Revelation*** | **Caecaltus Dusk** and Malcador's sigil | Found beside the proconsul's burned body. And the third nail plays too. The pentacle left in the Emperor's shoulder comes loose in his hand, "gripped like a chakram" (TEATD3 10:viii), a throwing blade, and cuts Horus with the nail Horus drove. When it shatters, it is "its Sigillite magic entirely spent" (TEATD3 10:viii). The third nail was Malcador's. That is the Mimir answer in reading 9.

Two more things turn up in the dust. Among the Emperor's cards Leetu finds one that was not in the spread Horus read: "*The World*" (TEATD3 10:xix). It is the last trump of the traditional tarot, the card of completion. Mixed in with those are Horus's own cards, "*The Orphan* and *The Revenger*. *The Despoiler*," and Leetu "doesn't say their names. He knows whose deck they came from" (TEATD3 10:xix). The Despoiler goes, in the book's last pages, to Abaddon.

So the thesis is literally what happens. Horus read the Emperor's hand as weak because he counted cards, not people. Every card is a servant or son whom the Emperor had placed on the board, and each of them wins a few seconds. He could not win alone. "He could not fight you alone. He could not beat you alone. But by bluff and ruse and stratagem and sacrifice, He has held your attention until He no longer has to" (TEATD3 10:xvi). That line answers the first thing the Emperor says in the trilogy: "*I cannot fight alone*" (TEATD1 1:xxiii).

**Myth: the tarot, card by card.** The **Imperial Tarot** of the setting is modelled on the real tarot. The historical deck began in fifteenth-century Italy as a card game, the *trionfi* or "triumphs," with an extra suit of picture cards that beat all the others. Our word "trump" comes from *trionfi*. The occult reading of the cards came later, in eighteenth-century France. There, the scholar Antoine Court de Gébelin claimed in *Le Monde primitif* (1781) that the trumps were a lost Egyptian book of wisdom. They were not, but the idea stuck. In the traditional order, the last of the twenty-one numbered trumps is **The World**: a figure dancing inside a wreath, surrounded by the four living creatures of Ezekiel and Revelation. It is the card of completion, the journey finished. Other trumps are close cousins of the Imperial cards: *The Emperor*, *The Tower* (once called *La Maison Dieu*, "the House of God"), and *Judgement*, where the dead rise at the angel's trumpet.

**Scripture: the mandate.** *Mandatio* is formed from the Latin *mandatum*, a charge or commandment. The word's most famous use is at the Last Supper: "A new commandment (*mandatum novum*) I give unto you, That ye love one another" (John 13:34). It gave the English church the name **Maundy Thursday**. Loken, the Knight of Mandatio, is the son who keeps that commandment to the end, staying behind alone with the father who betrayed him: "I am the only one left who cares" (TEATD3 10:xix).

**Correction.** An earlier version of this reading made Caecaltus the Knight of Mandatio and left the Guardsman and the Lantern unexplained. The Knight is Loken, by the verse title. Caecaltus is Revelation. The Guardsman is Oll and the Lantern is the Emperor, by Oll's own words.

## 13. Power, and the choice to set it down

**Thesis raised in discussion.** "Psychic" does not begin to describe the magnitude of what the Emperor does and can constantly do.

**In the story.** In Volume II the Chaos gods hurl everything they have at the Emperor in the warp. He takes it in, and it starts turning him into the thing the gods foretold, the Dark King. He refuses it and throws it away before the final duel begins. In Volume III he fights Horus with a fraction of that power, and loses, until something else fills him.

**The reading.**
- **He carries it in his body.** He is a conduit for "the burning wire of immaterial force," trained against for thirty thousand years (TEATD2 5:ix). When the gods open the warp on him, "so He drinks from it" (TEATD2 5:ix).
- **He becomes what the gods wanted.** The narration says it plainly: "Your Emperor is the Dark King" (TEATD2 7:xliv). He is "the brightest thing in the galaxy," and "the light that casts the shadow of the Dark King" (TEATD2 6:viii).
- **He sets it down, and cuts away the part that would stop him.** "The power of the Dark King is expelled and scattered" (TEATD2 8:xvi). "Mere mortal now, divinity cast aside" (TEATD2 8:xvi), he draws his sword. Malcador sees what else he did: "My lord and friend has broken off a part of his soul" (TEATD2 8:xvi), the part that held his compassion, so that it cannot stay his hand.
- **He parries with a place and a date.** Even so reduced, he blocks a blow by moving them both across space and time, to one of Horus's own memories: "This may be the very day on which you were found" (TEATD3 9:xxi).

The book tracks the question with one refrain, changed each time it comes back:

1. "It is not your father's power, which is modest compared to your limitless supply. It is what He *does* with it" (TEATD3 9:xxi). Horus, in the duel, admiring his father's technique.
2. "It's not your father's power. It's how He *steals* it" (TEATD3 10:iv). Horus, blasted across the Court by power his father drew out of him through "that tender, casual touch."
3. "It's not His power, it's where it comes from" (TEATD3 10:xvi). Horus, at the end, seeing a species behind his father.
4. "It's not the power, it's what you do with it. And you, fool, let it go" (TEATD3 10:xvi). Horus, turned on himself.

The fourth line is the Emperor's own choice in Volume II, turned into Horus's error in Volume III. The father let the power go on purpose and fought as a man. The son let it go because he was tricked, and died as one. Magnitude is the setup, not the point. The Dark King is what the gods wanted him to become. The sword in a mortal hand is his refusal. The light he fights with at the end is not his own, but his people's: "the pure white light of a species that, in its madness, believes in Him beyond all reason" (TEATD3 10:xvi).

**Scripture: kenosis.** In his letter to the Philippians (2:5–8), Paul describes Christ, "who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God: but made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant, and was made in the likeness of men." Theologians call this *kenosis*, from the Greek for "emptying": God choosing to set aside the powers of divinity and live as a man.

## 14. The Throne: the anti-theist argument turned around

**Thesis raised in discussion.** He still chose the Throne. The man who denied the gods became one, or embodied one, to attempt the salvation of humanity. It is the anti-theist argument turned around.

**In the story.** At the moment of the Emperor's last stand, his power comes from worship. Across Terra, led by the saint Euphrati Keeler in the Hollow Mountain, people are chanting one phrase. After Horus is dead, Dorn and Valdor carry the broken Emperor to the Golden Throne. Malcador, in his last moments, watches. And in the book's final pages, a woman walking with a column of convict soldiers tells them a story about a man called Ollanius.

**The reading.**
- **The worship he forbade saves him.** The whispers Horus has heard all along are prayer: "*Speak this with me, as it is spoken to me. The Emperor must live*" and "*Lift up your hands. He must live*" (TEATD3 10:xvi). They carry a line of scripture altered: "*To be absent in the body is to be present in the Emperor*" (TEATD3 10:xvi), from Saint Paul's "absent from the body, and … present with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8). The Emperor spent the Great Crusade suppressing exactly this. It is the one power that turns the duel. The book states the mechanism outright: humanity "imagines Him to be its shield and protector, and has such faith in that act of imagination, it is made real" (TEATD3 10:xvi). That is the atheist's account of God, word for word, and in this universe the account is true, and it works.
- **The Throne is a consoling misreading.** Malcador narrates the carrying: "He is the throne's only chance" (TEATD3 10:xx). Dorn and Valdor believe the Throne will heal the Emperor. It is the other way round, and Malcador chooses not to tell them: "The truth is brutal. At least, this way, they have some solace" (TEATD3 10:xx). That is the Imperial Creed being born. Loyal sons misread a sign as salvation, and the one man who knows the truth lets them. Abnett grants the anti-theist's whole mechanism, religion as a consoling misreading, and turns it. Here the misreading is mercy, not fraud, and its object is a real man, really suffering, really holding the door shut.
- **Then the legend.** In the last pages, a woman called Katerina Moriana walks with a column of convict gunners and tells them stories about a man who died holding his ground: "His name was Ollanius" (TEATD3 F:i). Her theory of it: "A good lie is better than a bad truth … Tell a good story, and you can restore things to the way they were. Tell a good lie, and you make them the way they should have been" (TEATD3 F:i). Oll, the Perpetual who spent forty thousand years refusing to believe in the Emperor, becomes, in the later setting, the saint **Ollanius Pius**, the lone guardsman who stood before Horus to protect his god.

The Emperor never denied that gods existed. He knew the four better than anyone. What he denied them was faith, because worship feeds them. The Imperial Truth was a noble lie, and he ran it from both ends. First he told humanity there were no gods, so it would not feed the four. Then he became a god, with the lie that the Throne was saving him.

Whether he chose it keeps its mask too: "it was a contingency … He had me believe" (TEATD3 10:xx). The installation is already liturgy, "on the brink of death and the verge of life" (TEATD3 10:xx). Malcador blesses it: "May your death live forever … There is nothing immortal about this" (TEATD3 10:xx).

**Philosophy and scripture: the noble lie, the sigh, the laying down.**
- **Plato's noble lie.** In the *Republic* (Book III), Socrates proposes that the ideal city be founded on a myth told to all its citizens: that they were born from the earth, with gold, silver or bronze mixed into their souls, which is why some rule and others work. The lie is noble because it holds the city together.
- **Marx on religion.** "Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world, and the soul of soulless conditions. It is the opium of the people." It comes from the introduction to his critique of Hegel's *Philosophy of Right* (1843–44). His point was that religion is a consolation for real suffering, and that the suffering is what has to be abolished.
- **The laying down.** "No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself" (John 10:18). Jesus is speaking of his own life.
- **The liturgy.** The Christian doxology, the *Gloria Patri*, ends: "as it was in the beginning, is now, and ever shall be, world without end."
- **Absent in the body.** "We are confident, I say, and willing rather to be absent from the body, and to be present with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8). Paul is writing about death: to leave the body is to be with Christ. The whispers keep the grammar and change the Lord to the Emperor.
- **Ollanius Pius.** In the wider setting, the legend of the Imperium's later ages is that a lone soldier, an ordinary guardsman named Ollanius Pius, stood between Horus and the fallen Emperor with only a lasgun, and died there. He is a saint of the Imperial Creed. For decades it was treated as pious fiction. The trilogy makes it true, gives the legend to Oll Persson, and shows the story being told for the first time.

## 15. Why it is serious fiction, and why wide reading pays

**Thesis raised in discussion.** The Heresy and the Siege deserve respect as serious fiction, not "war porn." And the more widely you have read, the more there is to see in Warhammer.

**In the story.** The end of the war is announced in a verse titled "Kairos and chronos": "This is the end and the death. But it is neither the end expected nor the death foretold" (TEATD3 10:xviii).

**The reading.**
- **It is tragedy in the strict sense.** Every reader knows the ending before page one, as every listener knew Troy would fall. Abnett says so in his afterword: "Everybody (pretty much) knew what was going to happen, so it *all had to happen*" (Afterword, TEATD3). All that is left is how and why, and the "neither … expected nor … foretold" line is the tragedian's answer. The central irony is Dostoevskian: a project to abolish religion produces the largest religion in history, with its author as the god, against his will.
- **The prose refuses war porn.** Putting Horus in the second person for three volumes implicates the reader in every choice. Abnett names the question himself: "Horus is eerily second-person, so we're close to him, but still held at bay (just who *is* talking to him?)" (Afterword, TEATD3). That is reading 6's question, asked by the author. The Emperor is written from outside on purpose: "He is communicated *by others*" (Afterword, TEATD3). The battles are summarised; grief gets the page. The serious claim rests on the series' best writers, Abnett, Wraight, Haley, French and Dembski-Bowden, not on every book. Commercial serialisation is how Dickens worked too. The one-line pitch: the Heresy is what happens when you give Satan the second person.
- **It was built by wide readers.** The Chaos of the setting is Michael Moorcock's, from his Eternal Champion novels, with Law against Chaos and the eight-pointed star. The entombed, all-seeing Emperor is Leto II from Frank Herbert's *God Emperor of Dune*. The Palace owes much to Mervyn Peake's *Gormenghast*. The plateau of Leng is Lovecraft's. And the whole Heresy is Milton's *Paradise Lost* with Milton's problem intact: the fallen angel gets the best lines. Abnett claims the density outright: "There's scarcely a line, or at least a paragraph, in the whole book that doesn't make some nod to something in the lore" (Afterword, TEATD3).
- **Wide reading also teaches how myths behave.** The trilogy says so itself: "Life's not like that, and neither are myths" (TEATD3 9:vi), with a reminder of Homer's heroes pausing to scrape their ships' hulls. A reader of Homer and of the Books of Samuel, where the same battle is told twice with different numbers, stops being angry at contradictions and feels the depth instead. Abnett's own method was to choose "Which version of it is the best to include (for in some cases there are many contradictory versions in 40K mythology)?" (Afterword, TEATD3). That is how a mythographer works.
- **A parody game can carry all this.** Once the warp is real, every theology in the setting is literally true, so anyone who takes it seriously ends up doing theology. The low form is where high material moves without being watched. Gene Wolfe put Catholic theology inside a torturer's memoir in *The Book of the New Sun*. Philip K. Dick put Gnostic revelation into paperback science fiction. The medieval mystery plays staged the Harrowing of Hell in market squares, with a smoking Hellmouth as a stage prop. The satire commits so completely to its own premise that it stops being a joke and becomes a mirror.

**Literature: the wide reading behind the setting.**
- **Michael Moorcock**'s Eternal Champion novels (from 1961) imagined a cosmic war between Law and Chaos. The eight-pointed star of Chaos, which Warhammer adopted, is his symbol.
- **Frank Herbert**'s *God Emperor of Dune* (1981) has Leto II, a ruler who has sacrificed his humanity to become a near-immortal, prescient tyrant, enduring millennia of suffering to steer humanity down the one path that saves it.
- **Mervyn Peake**'s *Gormenghast* novels (1946–59) describe a vast, crumbling castle ruled by incomprehensible ritual, the model for the Imperium's baroque decay.
- **H. P. Lovecraft** invented the Plateau of Leng and much of the cosmic horror the warp draws on.
- **John Milton**'s *Paradise Lost* (1667) retells the fall of Satan and of humanity. Readers since William Blake have noticed that Satan gets the most magnificent speeches, so that the poem seems to sympathise with the rebel it condemns.
- **Gene Wolfe**'s *The Book of the New Sun* (1980–83) is narrated by a torturer in a far-future world, and is also a deeply Catholic novel about a Christ figure. **Philip K. Dick**'s *VALIS* (1981) put Gnostic revelation into paperback science fiction.
- **The medieval mystery plays** were cycles of Bible stories staged by town guilds in market squares. Hell was a giant painted mouth, the **Hellmouth**, belching smoke and fireworks, and devils were played for laughs. The same plays staged the **Harrowing of Hell**, Christ breaking open hell to free the righteous dead.
- **Dostoevsky**, the Russian novelist, wrote murder thrillers that are also the deepest religious fiction of the nineteenth century: *Crime and Punishment*, *The Brothers Karamazov*.

**Time and tragedy.**
- **Kairos and chronos.** Greek has two words for time. *Chronos* is clock time, one moment after another. *Kairos* is the right moment, the time that is ripe. The New Testament uses *kairos* for the appointed hour: "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand" (Mark 1:15). In the trilogy, time has stopped in Horus's Court. When he dies, the verse that restarts it is titled "Kairos and chronos."
- **Tragedy.** Aristotle's *Poetics* describes the best tragic plots as ones where the audience knows the outcome, and the pleasure lies in *how* the reversal comes and in the *recognition* when a character sees the truth too late. The Greek terms are *peripeteia* and *anagnorisis*. Horus's recognition, on his knees, "You see it now, at last" (TEATD3 10:xvi), is the textbook example.
